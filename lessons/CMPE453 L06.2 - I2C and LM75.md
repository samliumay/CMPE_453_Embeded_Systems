# CMPE 453 · Lecture 6.2 · I2C: the two-wire interface (TWI)

> [!summary] In short
> - I2C (TWI) uses two wires shared by every device: SDA (data) and SCL (clock), pulled up to Vcc. One data wire means half duplex.
> - SDA changes only while SCL is low and is read while SCL is high. An SDA edge while SCL is high is START (falling) or STOP (rising).
> - Address byte = (7-bit address `<< 1`) `|` R/W, with 0 = write and 1 = read.
> - After every byte the **receiver** sends a ninth bit: 0 = ACK, 1 = NACK.
> - LM75: address `1001000`, write `0x90`, read `0x91`. MSB = degrees, LSB bit 7 = 0.5 °C.
> - $f_{SCL}=f_{CPU}/(16+2\cdot\text{TWBR}\cdot4^{\text{TWPS}})$. On the 16 MHz Uno `TWBR = 72` gives 100 kHz.

## 1. Two wires and the timing rules (slides 2-3)
![[cmpe453_lecture_6_lecture6_2_p002.jpg]]
![[cmpe453_lecture_6_lecture6_2_p003.jpg]]

**Notes.** I2C is a synchronous serial bus with two wires: **SCL** (serial clock) and **SDA** (serial data). Atmel calls its I2C hardware the 2-wire Serial Interface (**TWI**). On the Uno SDA = A4/PC4 and SCL = A5/PC5.

All devices connect to the same two wires. Two resistors pull SDA and SCL up to Vcc. A device can only pull a line **low**, so the idle bus is high.

The slide's question: there is only one data wire, so I2C is **half duplex**. The two sides take turns.

Timing rules (slide 3):
- A clock master controls the communication by sending the timing signal on SCL.
- The data line may change **only while the clock line is low**.
- Data is read **only while the clock line is high**.

The two exceptions are control signals:
- **START**: SDA goes from high to low while SCL is high.
- **STOP**: SDA goes from low to high while SCL is high.
- A START in the middle of a conversation, with no STOP before it, is a **repeated START**.

> [!info] Open-drain lines and the pull-ups R1, R2 (beyond the slides)
> - An I2C pin is **open-drain**: inside the device there is only a switch from the line to GND. The device can close the switch (line = 0) or open it (the device lets go). It has no switch to Vcc, so it can never drive a 1.
> - The 1 comes from the pull-up resistor. When every device lets go, R1 pulls SDA up to Vcc and R2 pulls SCL up to Vcc. Without the resistors a released line floats and never becomes a clean 1.
> - If one device pulls low and another lets go, the line is **low**. So a 0 always wins (a "wired AND"). No two outputs can fight each other. The only current is through the resistor: with Vcc = 5 V and a typical 4.7 kΩ pull-up, $I=5/4700\approx1.06$ mA.
> - This is why ACK is the low level: a device must actively pull SDA down to say "I am here".

> [!question]- Check (slide 2): The figure shows R1 and R2 between the bus and Vcc. Device 1 wants a 1 on SDA and device 2 wants a 0 at the same moment. Which level is on SDA, and why is nothing damaged? What happens to SDA if R1 is removed?
> SDA is **low**. Device 1 only lets go of the line, device 2 pulls it to GND, and a 0 always wins.
> Nothing is damaged because no device drives the line high. The only path from Vcc is R1, so the current is small (about 1 mA with 4.7 kΩ at 5 V).
> Without R1 nothing can make SDA high. A released line floats, so a 1 (and also START, STOP and NACK) cannot be sent.

> [!question]- Check (slide 3): In the figure, what happens in the blue bands and what happens in the green bands (B0, B1, ..., BN)? Why are the yellow Start and Stop bands not data bits?
> Blue bands: SCL is **low**. Here the sender may change SDA to the next bit (the crossing lines in the figure).
> Green bands: SCL is **high**. SDA is stable and the receiver reads the bit.
> In the yellow bands SDA changes while SCL is **high**. A data bit never does that, so every device recognises it as a control signal: falling SDA = START, rising SDA = STOP.

## 2. Addresses and the read/write bit (slide 4)
![[cmpe453_lecture_6_lecture6_2_p004.jpg]]

**Notes.** Several devices share the lines, so each device has a specific **7-bit address**. The **eighth bit** (R/W) says who sends next:
- START, address, then **0**: the slave is going to **receive** a byte from the AVR (write).
- START, address, then **1**: the slave **sends** data, the master reads it off the bus (read).

Recipe for the address byte: take the 7-bit address $a$, shift it left by one (`a << 1`), then write = `(a << 1) | 0`, read = `(a << 1) | 1`.

> [!example] LM75
> 7-bit address `1001000` = `0x48`. Write byte `0b10010000` = `0x90`. Read byte `0b10010001` = `0x91`.

> [!warning] Watch out
> - The slide says "Eight bit". It means the eighth bit of the address byte (bit 0, sent last).
> - Datasheets give addresses as 7-bit (`0x48`) or already shifted (`0x90`). Shifting twice, or not at all, is the most common reason why a device does not answer.

> [!question]- Check (slide 4): A display has the 7-bit address `0x3C`. Compute its address bytes for writing and for reading. Then decode the address byte `0xA1` seen on the bus: which 7-bit address, and who sends the next data byte?
> `0x3C` = `0111100`. Shift left by one: `01111000` = `0x78`.
> Write: `0x78 | 0` = **`0x78`**. Read: `0x78 | 1` = **`0x79`**.
> `0xA1` = `10100001`. Bit 0 is 1, so it is a **read**: the slave sends the next byte. The address is the upper 7 bits: `0xA1 >> 1` = `1010000` = **`0x50`**.

## 3. ACK or NACK (slide 5)
![[cmpe453_lecture_6_lecture6_2_p005.jpg]]

**Notes.** The **receiver** of a byte (master or slave) acknowledges it. A **ninth bit** is sent after every 8-bit byte:
- **0 (low) = ACK**: byte acknowledged, the sender may continue.
- **1 (high) = NACK**: there was an error, or the transmitter should stop sending, or the whole communication is about to end.

So a transmission error can be detected, for example in a noisy environment. In SPI everything is assumed to work.

A missing slave gives a NACK by itself: nobody pulls SDA low, the pull-up keeps it high.

> [!warning] Watch out
> A NACK is not always an error. The master sends a NACK on purpose after the last byte it wants to read.

> [!question]- Check (slide 5): A master sends the read address of a slave and then reads 3 data bytes. For each of the 4 bytes, say who sends the ninth bit and which value it has when everything works. How many SCL pulses are needed for the 4 bytes?
> The receiver of a byte sends the ninth bit.
> - Address byte: the master sends it, so the **slave** answers with ACK (0).
> - Data byte 1 and data byte 2: the slave sends them, so the **master** answers with ACK (0) to ask for more.
> - Data byte 3: the **master** answers with NACK (1). It means "this was the last byte", not an error.
>
> Each byte needs 8 data bits + 1 acknowledge bit = 9 pulses. $4\cdot9=36$ SCL pulses.

## 4. A real conversation: the AVR reads an LM75 (slides 6-9)
![[cmpe453_lecture_6_lecture6_2_p006.jpg]]
![[cmpe453_lecture_6_lecture6_2_p007.jpg]]
![[cmpe453_lecture_6_lecture6_2_p008.jpg]]
![[cmpe453_lecture_6_lecture6_2_p009.jpg]]

**Notes.** The LM75 is a temperature sensor. The AVR first sends a byte to put it in temperature-reading mode, then receives two bytes (here 21.5 degrees).

The conversation on the oscilloscope trace:

| Step | On the bus | Who sends |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | START (SDA falls while SCL is high) | AVR |
| 2 | `1001000` + `0` (write) = `0x90` | AVR |
| 3 | ACK (0) | LM75 |
| 4 | `00000000` (pointer: temperature register) | AVR |
| 5 | ACK (0) | LM75 |
| 6 | repeated START | AVR |
| 7 | `1001000` + `1` (read) = `0x91` | AVR |
| 8 | ACK (0) | LM75 |
| 9 | `00010101` = 21, the most significant byte | LM75 |
| 10 | ACK (0) | AVR |
| 11 | `10000000`, the least significant byte | LM75 |
| 12 | NACK (1): end of the communication | AVR |
| 13 | STOP (SDA rises while SCL is high) | AVR |

For the cheap LM75 only the first bit (bit 7) of the low byte counts: it stands for half a degree. So `00010101` and `10000000` mean $21+0.5=21.5$ °C.

Full format (datasheet): the temperature is a 9-bit two's-complement number in units of 0.5 °C:

$$T=\frac{(\text{MSB}\ll8\;|\;\text{LSB})\gg7}{2}\ ^\circ\text{C}\quad(\text{signed shift})$$

> [!info] The LM75 address pins A2, A1, A0 (beyond the slides)
> The LM75 has three address pins. Its 7-bit address is `1001` followed by the levels on A2, A1, A0 (GND = 0, Vcc = 1). The upper four bits `1001` are fixed in the chip.
> With all three pins on GND the address is `1001000` = `0x48`, as on the slides. Three pins give $2^3=8$ different addresses (`0x48` to `0x4F`), so up to 8 LM75s can share one bus.

> [!info] Why the AVR writes first: the pointer byte (beyond the slides)
> The LM75 has four registers: temperature (0), configuration (1), T_HYST (2), T_OS (3). These are the numbers in the `#define` lines on slide 11.
> The first data byte after the write address is the **pointer**: it selects the register for the next read. The slides call this "put it in temperature-reading mode". Pointer `00000000` selects the temperature register.

> [!info] The two temperature bytes, step by step (beyond the slides)
> The LM75 sends 9 useful bits: the 8 bits of the MSB and bit 7 of the LSB. Together they are one signed number $N$ (two's complement), and one step is 0.5 °C, so $T=N/2$.
> 1. Join the bytes into one 16-bit number: `(MSB << 8) | LSB`.
> 2. Read it as **signed**: if bit 15 is 1, subtract 65536.
> 3. Shift right by 7 (divide by 128). This drops the 7 unused low bits and leaves $N$.
> 4. Divide by 2.
>
> Positive, the slide's value `0x15 0x80`: `0x1580` = 5504. Bit 15 is 0. $5504/128=43$. $T=43/2=21.5$ °C.
> Negative, `0xE7 0x00`: `0xE700` = 59136. Bit 15 is 1: $59136-65536=-6400$. $-6400/128=-50$. $T=-50/2=-25$ °C.
> In C: `int16_t raw = (int16_t)((tempHighByte << 8) | tempLowByte);` then `raw >> 7` is $N$ (avr-gcc keeps the sign when it shifts a signed number right).
> Shortcut: read the MSB as a signed 8-bit number (whole degrees) and add 0.5 if bit 7 of the LSB is 1. For `0xE7`: $231-256=-25$, so −25 °C.

> [!question]- Check (slide 6): The AVR only wants to read the temperature. Why must it first send a byte to the LM75? Not counting the address bytes, how many bytes go from the AVR to the LM75 and how many come back?
> The LM75 has more than one register. The byte the AVR sends is the pointer: it tells the LM75 which register the next read will return. `00000000` selects the temperature register.
> **One** byte goes to the LM75 (the pointer). **Two** bytes come back (the temperature: most significant byte, then least significant byte).

> [!question]- Check (slide 7): A second LM75 on the same bus has its pins set to A2 = 0, A1 = 1, A0 = 1. Give its 7-bit address and its write and read address bytes. Which 9 bits does the AVR see on the bus right after START when it addresses this sensor for writing and the sensor answers?
> 7-bit address: `1001` + `011` = `1001011` = **`0x4B`**.
> Shift left by one: `10010110` = `0x96`. Write byte = **`0x96`**, read byte = `0x96 | 1` = **`0x97`**.
> On the bus: `1001011` (address), `0` (write), then `0` (ACK from the LM75). Together `100101100`.

> [!question]- Check (slide 8): After the pointer byte the AVR sends "another start command" and the address again. Why can it not simply start reading after the pointer byte? And why a repeated START and not a STOP followed by a new START?
> The direction of the transfer is fixed by the R/W bit, and this bit exists only in the address byte. The conversation began with R/W = 0 (the AVR sends). To make the LM75 send, the AVR needs a new address byte with R/W = 1 (`0x91`), and an address byte must come directly after a START.
> A repeated START does this without giving up the bus: no STOP means the bus never becomes free, so no other master can get in between the pointer write and the read. With only one master, STOP then START works too. It is only longer.

> [!question]- Check (slide 9): Convert these two readings to °C: (a) `00011110` then `00000000`, (b) `0xF5` then `0x80`.
> (a) `0x1E00` = 7680. Bit 15 is 0, so it is positive. $7680/128=60$. $T=60/2=$ **30.0 °C**. (Shortcut: MSB = 30, bit 7 of the LSB is 0.)
> (b) `0xF580` = 62848. Bit 15 is 1: $62848-65536=-2688$. $-2688/128=-21$. $T=-21/2=$ **−10.5 °C**.
> Shortcut for (b): MSB `0xF5` as a signed byte is $245-256=-11$. Bit 7 of the LSB is 1, so add 0.5: $-11+0.5=-10.5$ °C.
> The slide program would print (b) as `245.5`, because it treats the MSB as unsigned.

## 5. The TWI registers (slide 10)
![[cmpe453_lecture_6_lecture6_2_p010.jpg]]

**Notes.**

| Register | Job |
|---|---|
| `TWCR` | control: you command a step |
| `TWSR` | status: bits 7-3 are the status code (`TWSR & 0xF8`), bits 1-0 `TWPS1:0` the prescaler (1, 4, 16, 64) |
| `TWBR` | bit rate, an 8-bit number for the SCL formula |
| `TWDR` | the byte to send, or the byte received |

`TWCR` bits: `TWINT` (b7) step finished, `TWEA` (b6) send ACK when receiving, `TWSTA` (b5) make a START, `TWSTO` (b4) make a STOP, `TWEN` (b2) TWI on, `TWIE` (b0) interrupt.

$$f_{SCL}=\frac{f_{CPU}}{16+2\cdot\text{TWBR}\cdot4^{\text{TWPS}}}\qquad\text{TWBR}=\frac{f_{CPU}/f_{SCL}-16}{2\cdot4^{\text{TWPS}}}$$

Standard speeds: 100 kHz and 400 kHz. At 16 MHz with prescaler 1: `TWBR = 72` gives $16\,000\,000/160=100$ kHz, `TWBR = 12` gives 400 kHz.

> [!warning] Watch out
> `TWINT` is cleared by writing a **1** to it, not a 0. The 1 means "go". While `TWINT` is 1, SCL is held low and the bus waits.

> [!info] Reading the block diagram (beyond the slides)
> The slide shows only the datasheet figure. The thick boxes are the registers your program can use.
> - **Bus Interface Unit**: `TWDR` is a shift register. It shifts a byte out on SDA bit by bit (MSB first), or shifts a received byte in. This unit also makes START and STOP and handles the ACK bit.
> - **Bit Rate Generator**: makes SCL when the AVR is the master. It divides the CPU clock using `TWBR` and the prescaler. The prescaler bits `TWPS1:0` are in `TWSR`.
> - **Address Match Unit**: `TWAR` holds the AVR's **own** 7-bit address. It is used only when the AVR is a slave. The comparator checks every address byte on the bus against `TWAR`.
> - **Control Unit**: `TWCR` (your commands) and `TWSR` (the result of the last step).
>
> Where the SCL formula comes from: the hardware needs $16+2\cdot\text{TWBR}\cdot\text{prescaler}$ CPU clock cycles for one SCL period, with prescaler $=4^{\text{TWPS}}$ = 1, 4, 16 or 64. Frequency = $f_{CPU}$ divided by that number of cycles. A bigger `TWBR` or prescaler gives a slower SCL. After reset `TWPS1:0` = 00, so the prescaler is 1.

> [!question]- Check (slide 10, bit rate): $f_{CPU}=16$ MHz. (a) `TWBR = 18` and `TWPS1:0 = 01`. What is the SCL frequency? (b) Which `TWBR` gives 50 kHz with prescaler 1?
> (a) `TWPS = 1`, so the prescaler is $4^1=4$.
> $$f_{SCL}=\frac{16\,000\,000}{16+2\cdot18\cdot4}=\frac{16\,000\,000}{16+144}=\frac{16\,000\,000}{160}=100\ \text{kHz}$$
> The same speed as `TWBR = 72` with prescaler 1, because $2\cdot18\cdot4=2\cdot72\cdot1=144$.
> (b) $f_{CPU}/f_{SCL}=16\,000\,000/50\,000=320$.
> $$\text{TWBR}=\frac{320-16}{2\cdot1}=\frac{304}{2}=152$$
> 152 fits in the 8-bit register (maximum 255).

> [!question]- Check (slide 10, figure): Name the five registers in the thick boxes of the figure and the unit each one belongs to. Which of them does a master-only program, like the thermometer, never need, and why?
> - `TWDR`: Bus Interface Unit (address / data shift register).
> - `TWBR`: Bit Rate Generator.
> - `TWAR`: Address Match Unit.
> - `TWSR` and `TWCR`: Control Unit.
>
> A master-only program never needs `TWAR`. It holds the AVR's own address, and only a slave must recognise its address on the bus. The master puts the address of the other device into `TWDR`.

## 6. The I2C library and the thermometer program (slides 11-12)
![[cmpe453_lecture_6_lecture6_2_p011.jpg]]
![[cmpe453_lecture_6_lecture6_2_p012.jpg]]

**Notes.** Every step: (to send) put the byte in `TWDR`, write `TWCR` with `TWINT` = 1, `TWEN` = 1 and the extra bit for this step, wait until the hardware sets `TWINT` again, (to receive) read `TWDR`.

```c
void initI2C(void) {
  TWBR = 32;                      /* set bit rate, see p. 242 */
                                  /* 8MHz / (16+2*TWBR*1) ~= 100kHz */
  TWCR |= (1 << TWEN);            /* enable */
}
void i2cWaitForComplete(void) {
  loop_until_bit_is_set(TWCR, TWINT);
}
void i2cStart(void) {
  TWCR = (_BV(TWINT) | _BV(TWEN) | _BV(TWSTA));
  i2cWaitForComplete();
}
void i2cStop(void) {
  TWCR = (_BV(TWINT) | _BV(TWEN) | _BV(TWSTO));
}
uint8_t i2cReadAck(void) {
  TWCR = (_BV(TWINT) | _BV(TWEN) | _BV(TWEA));
  i2cWaitForComplete();
  return (TWDR);
}
uint8_t i2cReadNoAck(void) {
  TWCR = (_BV(TWINT) | _BV(TWEN));
  i2cWaitForComplete();
  return (TWDR);
}
void i2cSend(uint8_t data) {
  TWDR = data;
  TWCR = (_BV(TWINT) | _BV(TWEN));
  i2cWaitForComplete();
}
```

| Step | `TWCR` value |
|---|---|
| START | `TWINT\|TWEN\|TWSTA` = `0xA4` |
| send, or read with NACK | `TWINT\|TWEN` = `0x84` |
| read with ACK | `TWINT\|TWEN\|TWEA` = `0xC4` |
| STOP | `TWINT\|TWEN\|TWSTO` = `0x94` |

The event loop of the thermometer (slide 12), the same 13 steps as the trace:

```c
#define LM75_ADDRESS_W 0b10010000
#define LM75_ADDRESS_R 0b10010001
#define LM75_TEMP_REGISTER 0b00000000

i2cStart();
i2cSend(LM75_ADDRESS_W);
i2cSend(LM75_TEMP_REGISTER);
i2cStart();                        /* repeated START */
i2cSend(LM75_ADDRESS_R);
tempHighByte = i2cReadAck();
tempLowByte = i2cReadNoAck();
i2cStop();
printByte(tempHighByte);
if (tempLowByte & _BV(7)) {        //_BV(), defined in io.h
  printString(".5\r\n");
}
else {
  printString(".0\r\n");
}
_delay_ms(1000);                   /* Once per second */
```

> [!warning] Problems in the slide program
> - **The bit rate is for an 8 MHz chip** (the program comes from Williams' book). On the 16 MHz Uno `TWBR = 32` gives 200 kHz, not 100 kHz. It still works with the LM75. Use `TWBR = 72` for 100 kHz.
> - **No error checks.** `i2cSend` never looks at the ACK. If the LM75 is missing, the program prints junk as a temperature. Careful code checks `TWSR & 0xF8` after each step and sends a STOP on failure.
> - **Negative temperatures** print wrongly: `printByte` treats the MSB as unsigned, so −25 °C (`0xE7 0x00`) prints as `231.0`.
> - `tempLowByte && _BV(7)` (logical AND) would be true for any non-zero low byte. The test needs `&`.

> [!question]- Check (slide 11, read functions): `i2cReadAck` and `i2cReadNoAck` differ in one bit of `TWCR`. Which bit, and what is different on the bus? Why does every function also write `TWEN`, although `initI2C` has already set it?
> The bit is `TWEA` (bit 6). With `TWEA` = 1 the AVR pulls SDA low in the ninth clock after the received byte: **ACK**, "send me more". With `TWEA` = 0 the AVR leaves SDA high: **NACK**, "this was the last byte".
> The functions use `TWCR = ...`, which overwrites the whole register. A bit that is not in the value becomes 0. Without `_BV(TWEN)` the write would clear `TWEN` and switch the TWI hardware off.

> [!question]- Check (slide 11, i2cStop): Every function that writes `TWCR = ...` then calls `i2cWaitForComplete()`, except `i2cStop`. Why does `i2cStop` not wait for `TWINT`?
> After a STOP the hardware does **not** set `TWINT`. A STOP ends the conversation, so there is no next step to report. `loop_until_bit_is_set(TWCR, TWINT)` would then wait forever and the program would hang.
> The hardware clears `TWSTO` by itself when the STOP is on the bus.

> [!question]- Check (slide 12): A student swaps the two read calls: `tempHighByte = i2cReadNoAck(); tempLowByte = i2cReadAck();`. What does the LM75 do after the first byte, and what does the program print at 21.5 °C?
> After the first byte the AVR now sends a **NACK**. For the LM75 this means "stop sending", so it lets go of SDA.
> The second read is then no longer a valid I2C step. If the AVR still clocks in a byte, nobody pulls SDA low, the pull-up keeps it high, and the byte is most likely `11111111` = `0xFF` (expected bus behaviour, not tested on the board).
> With `tempLowByte` = `0xFF`, bit 7 is 1, so the program would print `.5` every time, also when the real temperature is 21.0 °C. `tempHighByte` is still 21. At 21.5 °C it prints `21.5`, which is correct only by luck.
> Rule: ACK every byte except the last one, NACK the last one.

## Key formulas and terms
| Term | Meaning / formula |
|---|---|
| Bus | SDA + SCL, pull-ups to Vcc, idle high, devices only pull low. Uno: A4/PC4, A5/PC5 |
| Timing | SDA changes while SCL is low, is read while SCL is high |
| START / STOP | SDA falls / rises while SCL is high |
| Frame | S, address (7) + R/W, A, data (8), A, ..., P. Sr = repeated START |
| Address byte | `(a << 1) \| 0` write, `(a << 1) \| 1` read |
| ACK / NACK | 9th bit from the receiver, 0 = ACK, 1 = NACK |
| LM75 | address `1001000`, W `0x90`, R `0x91`, pointer 0 = temperature, MSB = degrees, LSB bit 7 = 0.5 °C |
| SCL | $f_{CPU}/(16+2\cdot\text{TWBR}\cdot4^{\text{TWPS}})$. 16 MHz: 72 → 100 kHz, 12 → 400 kHz |
| One TWI step | write `TWCR = TWINT\|TWEN\|extra`, wait for `TWINT`, use `TWDR` |

## Check yourself
> [!question]- 1. I2C has one data wire and both sides may send on it. Half or full duplex? Why?
> **Half duplex.** One wire carries one level at a time, so the two sides take turns. Full duplex needs one wire per direction.

> [!question]- 2. SDA falls while SCL is high. What is it? And if SDA falls while SCL is low?
> While SCL is high: a **START**. While SCL is low: just a 0 bit being prepared.

> [!question]- 3. A sensor has the 7-bit address `0x48`. Give the address bytes for writing and reading.
> `0x48 << 1` = `0x90`. Write: **`0x90`**. Read: **`0x91`**.

> [!question]- 4. The LM75 returns `00010101` then `10000000`. What is the temperature? Who sends the ACK after the first byte and what follows the second?
> $21+0.5=$ **21.5 °C**. The AVR (the receiver) ACKs the first byte, then sends a NACK after the second and a STOP.

> [!question]- 5. `TWBR = 32`, prescaler 1. What is the SCL frequency at 8 MHz and at 16 MHz? Which `TWBR` gives 100 kHz at 16 MHz?
> 8 MHz: $8\,000\,000/(16+64)=$ **100 kHz**. 16 MHz: $16\,000\,000/80=$ **200 kHz**. For 100 kHz: $(160-16)/2=$ **72**.

> [!question]- 6. The LM75 is unplugged. What does the bus show after the address byte, and what does the slide program do?
> Nobody pulls SDA low in the ninth clock, so the master reads a **NACK**. The slide program does not check it and prints junk. Checked code sees the wrong status in `TWSR & 0xF8`, sends a STOP and reports an error.
