# CMPE 453 · Lecture 6.1 · SPI: the Serial Peripheral Interface

> [!summary] In short
> - SPI is a synchronous serial bus. The master makes the clock (SCK). One bit moves each way per clock pulse. No start or stop bits.
> - Four lines: MOSI (master → slave), MISO (slave → master), SCK, and SS (active low, one per slave).
> - On the Uno: SS = PB2/D10, MOSI = PB3/D11, MISO = PB4/D12, SCK = PB5/D13.
> - `SPCR` configures, `SPSR` reports (`SPIF` = done), `SPDR` is the byte: write = send, read = received.
> - Mode = $2\cdot\text{CPOL}+\text{CPHA}$. Master and slave must use the same mode and bit order.
> - Master and slave shift registers form a ring: after 8 clocks the two bytes have swapped.

## 1. What SPI is (slide 2)
![[cmpe453_lecture_6_lecture6_1_p002.jpg]]

**Notes.** SPI (Serial Peripheral Interface) is a **clocked** (synchronous) protocol for very fast transfer between two or more devices. Used for SD card and RFID reader modules, 2.4 GHz wireless modules, flash memory, sensors, real-time clocks, ADCs.

Speed: from 1 MHz, 10 MHz is "pretty much standard" (about a thousand times faster than UART), up to 50-100 MHz on fast chips.

Lines: a clock line, two data lines (one per direction), and a fourth line that signals the start and end of a transaction. The trade-off: speed, but more wires, and no acknowledge.

> [!info] On the Uno
> The ATmega328P's fastest SPI clock is $f_{osc}/2=8$ MHz. As a slave it works reliably only up to $f_{osc}/4=4$ MHz.

> [!question]- Check (slide 2): The slide says 10 MHz SPI is "a thousand times faster than UART". Test this against a 9600 baud UART (8N1): compare the bit rates and the time for one byte.
> Bit rates: SPI moves 1 bit per clock, so $10\,000\,000$ bit/s. UART: $9600$ bit/s. Ratio: $10\,000\,000/9600\approx 1042$. The claim is right.
> One byte over SPI: 8 clocks, $8/10\,000\,000\ \text{s}=0.8\ \mu\text{s}$.
> One byte over UART 8N1: 1 start + 8 data + 1 stop = 10 bits, $10/9600\ \text{s}\approx 1.042$ ms.
> Ratio per byte: $1.042\ \text{ms}/0.8\ \mu\text{s}\approx 1302$. It is even larger, because SPI has no start and stop bits.

## 2. The four pins (slides 3-4)
![[cmpe453_lecture_6_lecture6_1_p003.jpg]]
![[cmpe453_lecture_6_lecture6_1_p004.jpg]]

**Notes.**
- **MISO, Master In Slave Out**: input for the master, output for the slave. Data from slave to master.
- **MOSI, Master Out Slave In**: output for the master, input for the slave. Data from master to slave.
- **SCK**: the SPI clock. The master drives it.
- **SS, Slave Select**: active low. The master pulls it to 0 to talk to that slave and back to 1 at the end.

| Line | AVR pin | Uno pin |
|---|---|---|
| SS | PB2 | D10 |
| MOSI | PB3 | D11 |
| MISO | PB4 | D12 |
| SCK | PB5 | D13 |

> [!warning] Watch out
> - MOSI goes to MOSI and MISO to MISO. They are not crossed like UART TX/RX.
> - You must set the output pins in `DDRB` yourself. A master with `DDB3` and `DDB5` at 0 sends nothing.
> - D13 is also the on-board LED. It flickers with the clock, that is normal.

> [!question]- Check (slide 3): For each of the four lines say who drives it (puts the voltage on it): master or slave. How many lines does a slave drive?
> - MOSI: the master (Master **Out**).
> - SCK: the master, it makes the clock.
> - SS: the master, it selects the slave.
> - MISO: the slave (Slave **Out**).
>
> The slave drives only **one** line, MISO. The other three are inputs for the slave.

> [!question]- Check (slide 4): Read the SPI pins off the pinout. Then give the `DDRB` value (binary and hex) that makes SS, MOSI and SCK outputs and leaves MISO an input, with all other Port B pins as inputs.
> From the red box: SCK = PB5 (D13), MISO = PB4 (D12), MOSI = PB3 (D11), SS = PB2 (D10).
> Outputs are bits 5, 3 and 2: `(1<<5)|(1<<3)|(1<<2)` = `0b00100000 | 0b00001000 | 0b00000100` = `0b00101100` = **`0x2C`**.
> Bit 4 (MISO) stays 0, so it is an input.

## 3. The bus and the clock edges (slides 5-6)
![[cmpe453_lecture_6_lecture6_1_p005.jpg]]
![[cmpe453_lecture_6_lecture6_1_p006.jpg]]

**Notes.** The master generates SCK for all slaves. The master sets the voltage on MOSI, the slave on MISO. All chips share SCK, MISO and MOSI, but each slave needs its **own** slave-select line. So $n$ slaves need $3+n$ wires, which keeps SPI to small networks.

Clock edges (slide 6):
- On **one edge** each side sets up (puts out) its next bit.
- On the **next edge** each side reads (samples) the bit on its input.
- With every clock tick **both** sides transmit and receive one bit. There is no separate "send" and "receive" in SPI.

8 clocks = one byte sent and one byte received. To only read, the master sends a dummy byte.

> [!warning] Watch out
> Full duplex does not mean either side can start. Only the master makes clock pulses, so only the master starts a transfer.

> [!info] Why only one slave may be selected (beyond the slides)
> All slaves share the MISO wire. A slave whose SS is high releases MISO (its output is switched off, "high impedance") and ignores SCK and MOSI. Only the selected slave drives MISO. If two SS lines were low at once, two outputs would drive the same wire with different levels and the master would read garbage.

> [!question]- Check (slide 5): In the figure the master wants to talk to the DAC. What level must each of the three slave-select lines have? What goes wrong if two of them are low?
> SS of the DAC: **low** (0). SS of the EEPROM and of the ADC: **high** (1). SS is active low, so only the DAC is active.
> If two are low, both slaves put their bits on the shared MISO line at the same time. The two outputs fight and the master cannot read a valid bit.

> [!question]- Check (slide 6): Find the mistake: "First the master sends its 8 bits, then the slave answers with its 8 bits. So one byte exchange needs 16 clock pulses."
> There is no "first send, then answer". With every clock tick **both** sides transmit one bit and receive one bit. On one edge both put out a bit, on the next edge both read a bit.
> So 8 clock pulses move one byte from master to slave **and** one byte from slave to master. An exchange needs **8** pulses, not 16.

## 4. The SPI module and SPCR (slides 7-10)
![[cmpe453_lecture_6_lecture6_1_p007.jpg]]
![[cmpe453_lecture_6_lecture6_1_p008.jpg]]
![[cmpe453_lecture_6_lecture6_1_p009.jpg]]
![[cmpe453_lecture_6_lecture6_1_p010.jpg]]

**Notes.** `SPCR`, the SPI Control Register:

| Bit | Name | Meaning when 1 |
|---|---|---|
| 7 | `SPIE` | SPI interrupt enabled (needs `sei()` too) |
| 6 | `SPE` | SPI enabled. The normal I/O functions of its pins are overridden |
| 5 | `DORD` | LSB first (0 = MSB first) |
| 4 | `MSTR` | master, this device makes the clock (0 = slave) |
| 3 | `CPOL` | SCK is HIGH when idle (0 = LOW when idle) |
| 2 | `CPHA` | sample on the trailing edge (0 = leading edge) |
| 1-0 | `SPR1`, `SPR0` | clock rate select, with `SPI2X` |

SCK frequency (slide 10):

| `SPI2X` | `SPR1` | `SPR0` | SCK | at 16 MHz |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 0 | $f_{osc}/4$ | 4 MHz |
| 0 | 0 | 1 | $f_{osc}/16$ | 1 MHz |
| 0 | 1 | 0 | $f_{osc}/64$ | 250 kHz |
| 0 | 1 | 1 | $f_{osc}/128$ | 125 kHz |
| 1 | 0 | 0 | $f_{osc}/2$ | 8 MHz |
| 1 | 0 | 1 | $f_{osc}/8$ | 2 MHz |
| 1 | 1 | 0 | $f_{osc}/32$ | 500 kHz |
| 1 | 1 | 1 | $f_{osc}/64$ | 250 kHz |

> [!info] Reading the block diagram of slide 7 (beyond the slides)
> Slide 7 shows only the datasheet figure. Read it from the top left:
> - **XTAL → Divider**: the system clock $f_{osc}$ (16 MHz on the Uno) is divided by 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64 and 128.
> - **Select**: the bits `SPI2X`, `SPR1`, `SPR0` choose one of these divided clocks. This is the "SPI Clock (Master)". In slave mode this clock is not used: the clock comes in from the SCK pin.
> - **Clock Logic**: makes the SCK edges with the polarity and phase set by `CPOL` and `CPHA`.
> - **8 Bit Shift Register + Read Data Buffer**: together they are `SPDR`. The shift register sends and receives the bits. When a byte is complete it is copied into the read buffer, and a read of `SPDR` returns the buffer.
> - **Pin Control Logic**: connects the shift register to MOSI and MISO. The letters M and S show the path in master and in slave mode: a master shifts out on MOSI and in on MISO, a slave the other way round.
> - **SPI Status Register** (`SPSR`) and **SPI Control Register** (`SPCR`) are connected to the internal data bus, so the CPU can read and write them. `SPIF` also goes out as the **SPI Interrupt Request**.

> [!question]- Check (slide 7): In the block diagram three bit lines enter the "Select" block. Which bits are they, in which register is each, and what does "Select" do with them?
> The lines are `SPI2X`, `SPR1` and `SPR0`. `SPR1` and `SPR0` are bits 1 and 0 of `SPCR`. `SPI2X` is bit 0 of `SPSR`.
> The Divider makes $f_{osc}/2$ to $f_{osc}/128$. "Select" uses the three bits to pick one of these as the SPI clock of the master.

> [!question]- Check (slide 8): A program writes `SPCR = 0xC0;`. Which bits are 1? Is the device a master or a slave? What else is needed before an SPI interrupt routine can run?
> `0xC0` = `0b11000000`: bit 7 `SPIE` = 1 and bit 6 `SPE` = 1. SPI is enabled and the SPI interrupt is enabled.
> Bit 4 `MSTR` = 0, so the device is a **slave**.
> Still needed: the global interrupt enable (`sei()`), and an interrupt routine `ISR(SPI_STC_vect)`. `SPIE` is only the local enable.

> [!question]- Check (slide 9): `SPDR` = `0x4B` is sent with `DORD` = 1. In which order do the bits appear on the line? The receiver wrongly uses `DORD` = 0. Which byte does it read?
> `0x4B` = `01001011` (bit 7 ... bit 0). `DORD` = 1 sends the LSB first, so the line carries bit 0, bit 1, ..., bit 7: **1, 1, 0, 1, 0, 0, 1, 0**.
> A receiver with `DORD` = 0 takes the first bit as its bit 7: it builds `11010010` = **`0xD2`**. The byte is bit-reversed. Both sides must use the same bit order.

> [!question]- Check (slide 10): A sensor allows an SCK of at most 400 kHz. Which is the fastest allowed setting on the Uno (16 MHz)? Give `SPI2X`, `SPR1`, `SPR0` and the time for one byte.
> Possible clocks: $16\ \text{MHz}/2,4,8,16,32,64,128$ = 8 MHz, 4 MHz, 2 MHz, 1 MHz, 500 kHz, 250 kHz, 125 kHz.
> 500 kHz is too fast. The fastest one not above 400 kHz is $16\,000\,000/64=250\,000$ Hz = **250 kHz**.
> Bits: `SPI2X` = 0, `SPR1` = 1, `SPR0` = 0 (or `SPI2X` = 1, `SPR1` = 1, `SPR0` = 1, which is also $f_{osc}/64$).
> One bit takes $1/250\,000\ \text{s}=4\ \mu\text{s}$. One byte = 8 bits: $8\cdot 4\ \mu\text{s}=$ **32 µs**.

## 5. SPSR, SPDR and the data modes (slides 11-14)
![[cmpe453_lecture_6_lecture6_1_p011.jpg]]
![[cmpe453_lecture_6_lecture6_1_p012.jpg]]
![[cmpe453_lecture_6_lecture6_1_p013.jpg]]
![[cmpe453_lecture_6_lecture6_1_p014.jpg]]

**Notes.** `SPSR`, the SPI Status Register:
- Bit 7 `SPIF`: set to 1 when a serial transfer is complete.
- Bit 6 `WCOL`: write collision, `SPDR` was written during a transfer.
- Bit 0 `SPI2X`: double speed.

`SPDR`, the SPI Data Register: 8-bit read/write. **Writing** loads the byte to transmit and, in master mode, starts the transfer. **Reading** returns the byte received. With `DORD` = 1 bit 0 of `SPDR` goes first.

Data bits are shifted out and latched in on **opposite edges** of SCK, so the data has time to settle. The **leading** edge is the first edge of a clock pulse (leaving idle), the **trailing** edge the second.

| Mode | `CPOL` | `CPHA` | Leading edge | Trailing edge |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 0 | sample (rising) | setup (falling) |
| 1 | 0 | 1 | setup (rising) | sample (falling) |
| 2 | 1 | 0 | sample (falling) | setup (rising) |
| 3 | 1 | 1 | setup (falling) | sample (rising) |

Mode number = $2\cdot\text{CPOL}+\text{CPHA}$. The reset values give mode 0, MSB first.

> [!warning] Watch out
> - Slide 11 says `SPI2X` "reduces the frequency divider from 4x to 2x". It halves **every** divider: 4 → 2, 16 → 8, 64 → 32, 128 → 64.
> - `CPHA` = 0 does not mean "sample on the rising edge". It means the leading edge, which rises or falls depending on `CPOL`.

> [!info] Reading the timing diagram of slide 14 (beyond the slides)
> - The two SCK lines are the same pulses, one idle low (`CPOL` = 0, mode 0) and one idle high (`CPOL` = 1, mode 2).
> - The dashed vertical lines mark the **leading** edges. With `CPHA` = 0 the bit is sampled there, so the SAMPLE line has one short pulse at each dashed line: 8 pulses for 8 bits.
> - SS (drawn with a bar, active low) goes low before the first clock pulse and high after the last one.
> - With `CPHA` = 0 the first sample comes on the very first edge. So the first bit must be on the line **before** any clock edge: it is put out when the transfer starts. The later bits are set up on the trailing edges.
> - The two bottom rows name the bit under each sample: MSB, Bit 6, ..., LSB for `DORD` = 0, and LSB, Bit 1, ..., MSB for `DORD` = 1.

> [!question]- Check (slide 11): A program reads `SPSR` and gets `0x81`. What does this say? Which of the three named bits can the program write?
> `0x81` = `0b10000001`: bit 7 `SPIF` = 1, bit 6 `WCOL` = 0, bit 0 `SPI2X` = 1.
> So a transfer is complete, there was no write collision, and double speed is on.
> The Read/Write row of the slide shows R for `SPIF` and `WCOL` and R/W for `SPI2X`. The program can write only `SPI2X`. `SPIF` and `WCOL` are set by the hardware.

> [!question]- Check (slide 12): A master at $f_{osc}/16$ runs `SPDR = 0x7E;` and in the next line `x = SPDR;` with no wait. Why is `x` not the slave's answer? How long does the transfer take?
> Writing `SPDR` only **starts** the transfer. The 8 bits need 8 SCK periods.
> SCK = $16\ \text{MHz}/16=1$ MHz, so one bit takes $1\ \mu\text{s}$ and one byte $8\ \mu\text{s}$. That is $8\cdot 16=128$ CPU clock cycles.
> The read comes after only a few CPU cycles, so the new byte has not arrived yet and `x` holds an old value. The program must wait for `SPIF` = 1 first.

> [!question]- Check (slide 13): A sensor datasheet says: "SCK is high when idle. Data is sampled on the rising edge." Which mode is this? Give the value of `SPCR` for a master in this mode, MSB first, $f_{osc}/4$, no interrupt.
> Idle high: `CPOL` = 1. The clock leaves idle by falling, so the leading edge is falling and the trailing edge is rising.
> Sampling on the rising edge = on the trailing edge: `CPHA` = 1. Mode $2\cdot 1+1=$ **3**. The table row 3 agrees: setup (falling), sample (rising).
> `SPCR = (1<<SPE)|(1<<MSTR)|(1<<CPOL)|(1<<CPHA)` = `0x40 | 0x10 | 0x08 | 0x04` = `0b01011100` = **`0x5C`**. `DORD`, `SPR1`, `SPR0` and `SPIE` stay 0.

> [!question]- Check (slide 14): The diagram has two SCK lines (mode 0 and mode 2) but only one SAMPLE line. Why is one enough? In mode 2, is the data sampled on a rising or a falling edge?
> Both modes have `CPHA` = 0, so both sample on the **leading** edge. The leading edges of the two clocks are at the same moments (the dashed lines). Only the direction is different.
> Mode 0 idles low, so its leading edge is rising. Mode 2 idles high, so its leading edge is **falling**: mode 2 samples on the falling edge.

## 6. Inside: shift registers (slides 15-17)
![[cmpe453_lecture_6_lecture6_1_p015.jpg]]
![[cmpe453_lecture_6_lecture6_1_p016.jpg]]
![[cmpe453_lecture_6_lecture6_1_p017.jpg]]

**Notes.** A shift register is a row of 1-bit cells. At each clock every bit moves one place, a new bit enters at one end and one falls out at the other. **Serial in, parallel out**: bits arrive one by one, the CPU reads all 8 at once. **Parallel in, serial out**: the CPU writes 8 bits at once, they leave one by one.

Master and slave each have an 8-bit shift register. Together they form a ring (slide 17):
1. Both registers are parallel-loaded with the data to send (example: slave all zeros, master all ones).
2. 8 clocks shift the bits around the ring.
3. Each processor reads its register: it now holds the other side's byte.
4. The registers are reloaded for the next byte.

> [!example] Master `0xA5`, slave `0x3C`, mode 0, MSB first
> Each clock: both registers shift one place left, the other side's old bit 7 enters at bit 0.
> - Start: master `10100101`, slave `00111100`
> - Clock 1: master `01001010`, slave `01111001`
> - Clock 2: master `10010100`, slave `11110010`
> - Clock 3: master `00101001` = `0x29`
> - After 8 clocks: master `0x3C`, slave `0xA5`. The bytes have swapped.

> [!info] How to read the three figures (beyond the slides)
> - **Slide 15**: the numbers 0 to 7 in the boxes are cell positions, not data. The MOSI arrow leaves the master register at one end and enters the slave register. The MISO arrow leaves the slave register and enters the master register at the other end. The two 8-bit registers are one 16-bit ring. The falling step on the SS line means: SS goes low, the slave is selected.
> - **Slide 16**: each row is the same register one clock later. The bit at the red arrow enters on the left, all bits move one cell to the right, the rightmost bit falls out. "Universal register" means the SPI register does both jobs: the CPU loads it in parallel (`SPDR = x`) and reads it in parallel (`y = SPDR`), and the line side is serial.
> - **Slide 17**: the master register shifts to the right and sends on MOSI. The slave register shifts to the left and sends on MISO. The figure shows the state **after one clock**: one 0 from the slave is in the master and one 1 from the master is in the slave.

> [!question]- Check (slide 15): After 8 clocks master and slave have swapped their bytes. What would each register hold if the master made 16 clocks and nobody reloaded the registers? Why?
> Each would hold its **own original byte** again.
> The two 8-bit registers form one ring of 16 cells. Each clock moves every bit one cell along the ring. After 8 clocks each bit is 8 cells further, which is the same position in the other register. After 16 clocks each bit has gone once around the full ring and is back where it started.

> [!question]- Check (slide 16): The register of the slide is empty (all 0). The serial bits 0, 1, 1, 0 arrive in this order, entering on the left as on the slide. Write the register after each of the 4 clocks.
> Rule for one clock: new register = the new bit, followed by the old first 7 cells.
> - Clock 1 (in 0): `00000000`
> - Clock 2 (in 1): `10000000`
> - Clock 3 (in 1): `11000000`
> - Clock 4 (in 0): `01100000`
>
> The first bit received (the 0 that is now in the 4th cell from the left) is the one furthest to the right. The bit received last is in the leftmost cell.

> [!question]- Check (slide 17): The example starts with the master all ones and the slave all zeros. The figure shows master `01111111` and slave `00000001`. How many clocks have passed? What do the registers hold 3 clocks later?
> **One** clock. The master shifted right and took one 0 from MISO on its left. The slave shifted left and took one 1 from MOSI on its right.
> - Clock 2: master `00111111`, slave `00000011`
> - Clock 3: master `00011111`, slave `00000111`
> - Clock 4: master `00001111`, slave `00001111`
>
> After 4 clocks half of each byte has moved over. After 8 clocks the master is `00000000` and the slave `11111111`.

## 7. SPI in C (slides 18-19)
![[cmpe453_lecture_6_lecture6_1_p018.jpg]]
![[cmpe453_lecture_6_lecture6_1_p019.jpg]]

**Notes.** The slide's code:

```c
// Initialize SPI Master Device (with SPI interrupt)
void spi_init_master(void)
{
  DDRB = (1<<5)|(1<<3);                            // Set MOSI, SCK as Output
  SPCR = (1<<SPE)|(1<<MSTR)|(1<<SPR0)|(1<<SPIE);   // master, Fosc/16, interrupt
  sei();
}
void spi_init_slave(void)
{
  DDRB = (1<<4);      // MISO as OUTPUT
  SPCR = (1<<SPE);    // Enable SPI
}
unsigned char spi_tranceiver(unsigned char data)
{
  SPDR = data;                       // Load data into the buffer
  while (!(SPSR & (1<<SPIF)));       // Wait until transmission complete
  return (SPDR);                     // Return received data
}
```

The wait loop (slide 19): while `SPIF` = 0, `SPSR & (1<<SPIF)` is 0 and `!(0)` is TRUE, so it keeps waiting. When `SPIF` = 1 the AND gives 128 and `!(128)` is FALSE, so the loop ends.

Exchange one byte as master: pull SS low, write `SPDR`, wait for `SPIF`, read `SPDR` (this clears `SPIF`), pull SS high.

> [!warning] Two bugs in the slide's master init
> 1. **`SPIE` without an interrupt routine.** No `ISR(SPI_STC_vect)` exists, so after the first transfer the CPU jumps to the default handler and **the program restarts**. Use polling **or** the interrupt, never `SPIE` alone.
> 2. **SS left as an input.** `DDRB = 0b00101000` leaves PB2 an input. In master mode a low level on SS clears `MSTR` and the AVR silently becomes a slave. Also `DDRB =` overwrites the other pins: use `|=`.

Corrected version:

```c
void spi_init_master(void)
{
  DDRB  |= (1 << PB2) | (1 << PB3) | (1 << PB5);  /* SS, MOSI, SCK out */
  PORTB |= (1 << PB2);                            /* SS high = no slave selected */
  SPCR = (1 << SPE) | (1 << MSTR) | (1 << SPR0);  /* mode 0, MSB first, f/16 */
}
uint8_t spi_transceive(uint8_t data)
{
  SPDR = data;                                    /* start the exchange */
  while (!(SPSR & (1 << SPIF)))                   /* 8 clocks later SPIF = 1 */
    ;
  return SPDR;                                    /* read clears SPIF */
}
```

A slave's answer is the byte it loaded **before** the exchange.

> [!info] How `SPIF` is cleared (beyond the slides)
> `SPIF` is read-only, the program cannot write it to 0. The hardware clears it in one of two ways: when the SPI interrupt routine starts, or when the program reads `SPSR` while `SPIF` is 1 and then accesses `SPDR`. The polling function does exactly this: the `while` loop reads `SPSR`, then `return SPDR` reads the data register. So the flag is 0 again before the next byte.

> [!question]- Check (slide 18, master): Compute the value that the slide's `spi_init_master` writes to `SPCR` (binary and hex). Which SPI mode, bit order and SCK frequency does it give on the Uno?
> `(1<<SPE)|(1<<MSTR)|(1<<SPR0)|(1<<SPIE)` = `0x40 | 0x10 | 0x01 | 0x80` = `0b11010001` = **`0xD1`**.
> `CPOL` = 0 and `CPHA` = 0: mode $2\cdot 0+0=$ **0**. `DORD` = 0: **MSB first**.
> `SPR1:0` = 01 and `SPI2X` = 0 (reset value): $f_{osc}/16=16\ \text{MHz}/16=$ **1 MHz**. This is the row framed in red on the slide.

> [!question]- Check (slide 18, slave): The slide's `spi_init_slave` makes only PB4 an output and does not set `SPR1`, `SPR0` or `MSTR`. Why is that enough for a slave?
> A slave drives only MISO, which is PB4, so only this pin must be an output: `DDRB = (1<<4)` = `0x10`. MOSI, SCK and SS are inputs for a slave.
> `MSTR` = 0 is the slave setting, and 0 is the reset value. The slave makes no clock, it receives SCK from the master. So the clock rate bits `SPR1`, `SPR0` have no effect in a slave.
> Only `SPE` is needed: `SPCR = (1<<SPE)` = `0x40`.

> [!question]- Check (slide 19): Evaluate the loop condition `!(SPSR & (1<<SPIF))` for `SPSR` = `0x01` and for `SPSR` = `0x81`. Why would `while (SPSR == 0);` be a wrong wait loop here?
> `(1<<SPIF)` = `(1<<7)` = `0b10000000` = `0x80`.
> - `SPSR` = `0x01`: `0x01 & 0x80` = `0x00`, and `!(0)` is TRUE. The loop keeps waiting. Correct, because `SPIF` = 0.
> - `SPSR` = `0x81`: `0x81 & 0x80` = `0x80` = 128, and `!(128)` is FALSE. The loop ends. Correct, because `SPIF` = 1.
>
> In both cases `SPI2X` (bit 0) is 1. The AND mask keeps only bit 7, so the other bits do not disturb the test. `while (SPSR == 0);` tests all 8 bits: with `SPSR` = `0x01` it would stop at once, before the transfer is complete.

## Key formulas and terms
| Term | Meaning / formula |
|---|---|
| Lines | MOSI PB3/D11, MISO PB4/D12, SCK PB5/D13, SS PB2/D10 (active low) |
| Wires for $n$ slaves | $3+n$ |
| `SPCR` | `SPIE` b7, `SPE` b6, `DORD` b5 (1 = LSB first), `MSTR` b4, `CPOL` b3, `CPHA` b2, `SPR1:0` b1:0 |
| `SPSR` | `SPIF` b7 transfer complete, `WCOL` b6, `SPI2X` b0 double speed |
| `SPDR` | write = load and start (master), read = received byte |
| SCK | `SPR1:0` = 00, 01, 10, 11 give $f/4$, $f/16$, $f/64$, $f/128$. `SPI2X` halves each |
| Mode | $2\cdot\text{CPOL}+\text{CPHA}$. `CPOL`: idle level. `CPHA`: 0 = sample on leading edge |
| Exchange | SS low, write `SPDR`, wait `SPIF`, read `SPDR`, SS high |

## Check yourself
> [!question]- 1. How many wires does a master need for 3 slaves? Are MOSI and MISO crossed?
> $3+3=$ **6** (SCK, MOSI, MISO and three SS lines), plus GND. Not crossed: MOSI to MOSI, MISO to MISO.

> [!question]- 2. Master `SPDR` = `0xA5`, slave `SPDR` = `0x3C`, mode 0, MSB first. Master register after 3 clocks? Both after 8?
> After 3 clocks the master holds `00101001` = **`0x29`**. After 8 clocks they have swapped: master **`0x3C`**, slave **`0xA5`**.

> [!question]- 3. Which SPI mode has `CPOL` = 1, `CPHA` = 0? On which edge is data sampled?
> Mode $2\cdot1+0=$ **2**. Sample on the leading edge, which is **falling** because the clock idles high.

> [!question]- 4. What SCK frequency does `SPCR = (1<<SPE)|(1<<MSTR)|(1<<SPR0);` give on the Uno? And with `SPI2X` set as well?
> `SPR1:0` = 01: $f_{osc}/16=$ **1 MHz**. With `SPI2X`: $f_{osc}/8=$ **2 MHz**.

> [!question]- 5. The slide's `spi_init_master` plus `spi_tranceiver`: the program restarts after the first byte. Why?
> `SPIE` and `sei()` are set but there is no `ISR(SPI_STC_vect)`. When `SPIF` becomes 1 the CPU jumps to the default handler, which jumps to address 0. Fix: leave out `SPIE` and `sei()` for polling. Also make PB2 (SS) an output.

> [!question]- 6. The master wants to read one byte from a slave. Write the line.
> `uint8_t b = spi_transceive(0xFF);` A read is an exchange with a dummy byte.
